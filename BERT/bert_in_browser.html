BERT in your browser: the model that reads both ways

BERT (Google, 2018) changed NLP almost overnight. Below you can run it on three classic tasks — filling in a blank word, judging the sentiment of a review, and finding names of people, places and organisations. Everything runs on your own machine: each model is downloaded once into your browser the first time you press its button.

What is BERT?

BERT stands for Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers. Where GPT-2 is a decoder that reads left to right and predicts the next word, BERT is an encoder: it looks at the whole sentence at once, and every word can attend to the words on its left and its right. That makes it bad at writing text, but very good at understanding it.

GPT-2 · left to right
The bank of the ??? was flooded

Only sees the past. “bank” could still be money or a riverbank.

BERT · both directions
The bank of the [MASK] was flooded

Sees “flooded” too, so “river” becomes the obvious answer.

BERT learned language by playing a fill-in-the-blank game on Wikipedia and a corpus of books: 15% of the words were hidden behind a [MASK] token and the model had to guess them. This is called masked language modelling. The pre-trained model can then be fine-tuned: you put a small extra layer (a “head”) on top and train briefly on a labelled dataset for one specific task. The three demos below are exactly that — one pre-trained BERT body, three different heads.

1 · Fill-mask pre-training task

This is the raw, pre-trained BERT without any fine-tuning. Put [MASK] somewhere in a sentence and BERT ranks the words that fit best — using context on both sides of the gap.

Try:
Model: bert-base-uncased — about 110 MB on first use.

Compare the two “bank” sentences: the words after the gap decide the answer. The doctor sentence is a well-known way to expose the gender bias BERT absorbed from its training text.

2 · Text classification fine-tuned

Here BERT was fine-tuned on product reviews to predict a rating of 1 to 5 stars. The multilingual version was pre-trained on 104 languages, so it also understands Dutch, German, French, Spanish and Italian reviews.

Try:
Model: bert-base-multilingual-uncased-sentiment — about 170 MB on first use.

Try “Not bad at all” — a bag-of-words model sees “bad” and gets confused, BERT reads the negation in context.

3 · Named entity recognition fine-tuned

Instead of one label for the whole text, this head gives a label to every token: is it part of a person, an organisation, a location, a miscellaneous name — or nothing? This model was fine-tuned on English news (CoNLL-2003).

Try:
Model: bert-base-NER — about 110 MB on first use.

Note the Ajax example: is “Ajax” a club (ORG) or something else? The model has to decide from context alone.

One model, many jobs

The three demos share the same architecture: 12 transformer layers, 768-dimensional vectors, about 110 million parameters. What differs is the small head on top:

  • fill-mask — for each position, a score for every one of the 30,522 words in the vocabulary. This is the head BERT was pre-trained with.
  • classification — one vector for the whole text (the special [CLS] token at the start) goes into a layer with 5 outputs: 1 to 5 stars.
  • token classification — every token's vector goes into a layer with 9 outputs (B-PER, I-PER, B-ORG, … and O for “not a name”).

This recipe — pre-train once on a mountain of unlabelled text, then fine-tune cheaply on a small labelled dataset — is transfer learning, and BERT made it the default way to do NLP. You did not need Google's compute budget any more; a single GPU and a few thousand labelled examples were enough to beat the previous state of the art. Search engines, spam filters and many chatbot intent classifiers still run on BERT-style encoders today, because they are small, fast and precise.

How this page runs

Just like the GPT-2 page, this uses Transformers.js, which runs ONNX exports of the models in WebAssembly inside a Web Worker. Each model is quantised to 8-bit and fetched from the Hugging Face CDN the first time you use it, then kept in your browser's cache. No server, no API key: your text never leaves your laptop.
